\begin{center}\textbf{\normalsize Abstract}\end{center}\vspace{-0.4em}
\noindent The tools that a person needs to plan irrigation on one field are usually separate programs: a map, a source of satellite data, a crop-water calculator, a hydraulic design sheet, a simulation model, each with its own inputs and none of them showing where its numbers come from. This article describes SWAFL Desktop, an open, offline-first suite that joins these steps in one application and keeps one rule throughout: every number leads to a numbered equation with a provenance label (literature, data-driven or engineered). The suite has decision layers in several groups (irrigation design for surface, drip and sprinkler systems; irrigation control; mulch and evaporation; soil structure; deterministic phenotyping; field water balance; seasonal data; crops; feedback loops), further layers that show Daisy results, a library of crops with many crop-and-density combinations, and tools for the real field: a sketch of the field and the irrigation system, satellite vegetation indices from Sentinel, image analysis of the satellite photograph, water sources, terrain and soil from open data, management zones, zone-by-zone variable-rate irrigation, a field report and prescription files. The computing engines are the meta-model of a large Daisy ensemble, a daily layered water balance and closed-form hydraulic modules, and a graphical workbench runs Daisy itself (described in the companion paper on the workbench). A virtual phenotyping experiment grows one plant of a chosen crop and genotype in the soil, weather and management of the project, deterministically, well-watered and under drought. We report what was tested for this release. All layers open and run in a Chromium browser without a script error, and each produces a dashboard with results, charts, a design or figure tab and messages. The crop library is internally consistent except for some evergreen combinations whose mid-season coefficient is below the initial one, which follows the source values. The phenotyping model is exactly reproducible between runs and closes its soil water balance; under a drought pulse the genotypes lose different shares of grain mass, and water-saving, deep-rooting and stay-green genotypes lose least. No field validation is claimed. The satellite and open-data services could not be reached in the environment of the tests, so these paths were exercised on generated test data only.

\medskip\noindent \textbf{Keywords.} decision support; irrigation design and scheduling; Sentinel-2 vegetation indices; field sketch; plant phenotyping; crop water requirement; offline software; equation traceability; open science\par}

\section{Introduction}
Irrigation planning on a farm combines at least five kinds of work. The field has to be found, outlined and described (its area, slope, soil, water source). Its crop has to be monitored, today by satellite. The irrigation system has to be designed and checked hydraulically. The schedule (when, how much) has to be calculated from the weather, the crop and the soil. And the decision has to be put against what a process model says would happen if the soil, the weather or the management were different. Each kind of work has good tools: web maps and the Sentinel-2 archive \citep{Drusch2012}, crop-water calculators built on the FAO procedures \citep{Allen1998,Doorenbos1979}, hydraulic design methods \citep{Keller1990,Walker1989}, simulation models for crops and soil water \citep{Jones2003,Keating2003,Steduto2009,Simunek2008,Hansen2012}. They rarely share inputs, they often need a connection to the internet or a licence, and in many of them the path from an input to an output cannot be followed.

This article describes one application that joins them and the tests that were made of it for release 1.1.0. Its design goals are four. \emph{Offline first}: the core computes without a connection; open data (satellite scenes, maps, weather, soil, elevation) are used when a connection exists, and the program says what it used and what it could not reach. \emph{Traceable}: every output leads to a numbered equation, and every equation carries a label of its origin, so that a reader can tell what is taken from the literature, what was fitted to data, and what was designed for the software. \emph{One project}: the soil, crop, weather, mulch, irrigation and date are set once and every layer uses them. \emph{Open}: the source, the manual, the data and the check scripts are public (Software, data and code availability).

The software paper of the first release (Paper~2) described the meta-model, the daily model and the closed-form design modules and verified them. Release 1.1.0 adds a graphical workbench for Daisy and its connection to the decision layers (Paper~3), a deterministic phenotyping layer, a library of 48 crops, a sketch tool, tools for the real field with satellite indices, variable-rate irrigation and a field report. This article is the description of the whole and the report of what was tested.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) The architecture of a suite in which the field, the design, the schedule, the process model and the phenotyping share one project and one rule of traceability. (2) The tools for the real field and the way they fall back when the data are not available. (3) A deterministic phenotyping layer and its tests. (4) A crop library with a stated source of every coefficient. (5) A test of the whole suite (all layers opened and run; the crop library; the phenotyping model) and a statement of what was not tested.

\paragraph{What is new.} The new element is the combination, under one rule of traceability: a field sketch, Sentinel-2 indices and image analysis, management zones with zone-by-zone irrigation, closed-form design of three irrigation systems, a 48-crop library with a stated source for every coefficient, a daily water balance, the Daisy workbench, and a deterministic virtual phenotyping layer that grows a plant in the soil, weather and management of the project, well-watered and under drought, without random numbers. The tests that go with it (all 22 layers run, crop constraints, repeatability and mass closure of the phenotyping model) are reported together with the limits of what they show.

\paragraph{Weather as a choice.} The weather of a project is chosen from five sources and carries into every layer and into the Daisy runs: the built-in benchmark record (Taastrup, Denmark), seven bundled Daisy stations (Taastrup, Foulum and Jyndevad in Denmark, Bologna in Italy, Jadwisin in Poland, Bratislava in Slovakia and Valecov in the Czech Republic), a file of the user (CSV, Excel, Daisy DWF, DLF or DAI), online weather of any place on Earth from the Open-Meteo archive (ERA5 based; complete years only) and DMI open data with the user's own key. Any working date from 1980 to 2100 can be set in the toolbar: dates up to 1999 use the recorded baseline, later dates are projected from a baseline year that the user chooses (typical, dry, wet or a specific year), and projections are simulations and not forecasts. The online sources could not be reached in the environment of the tests.

\section{The suite}
\subsection{Architecture}
\Cref{fig:system} shows the architecture. The user works in one window with a tree of layers on the left, the picture or design of the layer in the middle, a dashboard on the right and a panel of project properties. The dashboard of every layer has the same five tabs (results, design, charts, figure, messages), so that a result is read in the same place whichever layer made it. A single Run button starts a layer; the results of a layer wait for a new run when a setting of the project is changed, so that nothing moves while the settings are being made. The date of the toolbar is the working date of the whole suite and can be set to any day from 1980 to 2100: the weather of 1980 to 1999 is a recorded baseline, that of 2000 to 2100 is projected from a chosen baseline year (a typical, dry, wet or specific year), and the user is told that projections are simulations and not forecasts.

\Cref{tab:layers} lists the 22 layers. The first three are closed-form design modules for surface (border, furrow, basin), drip and sprinkler irrigation, with a layout drawing, a pipe network, pump and hydraulic grade line, design checks, an optimiser for the surface layout and a schedule that ends in a message on when to irrigate with the interval, the application efficiency and the storage efficiency. \subsection{Traceability}
The manual of the release has about 470 numbered equations and 482 statements of provenance. Counted by label, 159 are marked as literature, 69 as data-driven and 287 as engineered (some equations carry two labels). A reader of a layer is told which equation produced a number, and which of the three origins it has. The label ``engineered'' means that the form of the relation was chosen for the software and has not been fitted to data or taken from a source; it is the largest class, and it is the class for which the verification of this article and of Paper~2 matters most.

\section{The real field}
\subsection{Sketch}
The field sketch is a drawing tool in which the user states the field (its corners on a grid or a rectangle by length and width, the ground slope, the direction of the laterals or furrows), places the water source, the inlet, the greenhouse, plant beds and areas that are not irrigated, and gets the area, the perimeter and the irrigated area. A five-step question list leads from the field to the crop, soil, water system and running; the sketch becomes the design of a layer with one click.

\subsection{Satellite indices and image analysis}
When the field is demarcated on the map (Esri World Imagery, OpenStreetMap), the suite searches the Sentinel-2 L2A catalogue (Element 84 Earth Search, with Microsoft Planetary Computer as the second source), reads the bands B02, B04, B05, B08 and B11 for the field only, removes the baseline offset and the pixels of cloud, shadow and snow with the scene classification, and computes for every 10\,m cell
\begin{equation}\label{eq:idx}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{NDVI}&=\frac{\rho_N-\rho_R}{\rho_N+\rho_R}, & \mathrm{EVI}&=2.5\,\frac{\rho_N-\rho_R}{\rho_N+6\rho_R-7.5\rho_B+1}, & \mathrm{SAVI}&=\frac{(1+L)(\rho_N-\rho_R)}{\rho_N+\rho_R+L},\\
\mathrm{NDRE}&=\frac{\rho_N-\rho_{RE}}{\rho_N+\rho_{RE}}, & \mathrm{NDWI}&=\frac{\rho_N-\rho_{SW}}{\rho_N+\rho_{SW}} & &(L=0.5),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
after \citet{Rouse1974}, \citet{Huete2002}, \citet{Huete1988}, \citet{Barnes2000} and \citet{Gao1996}. From the photograph of the field the visible-band indices VARI, GLI, ExG and NGRDI are averaged \citep{Gitelson2002,Woebbecke1995}, and a land cover (dense and sparse vegetation, bare soil, water, buildings and roads, shadow) is read by $k$-means clustering of six colour and texture features followed by named rules; this is classical computer vision and not a trained network, and the label of the equation says so. The field is split into five management zones by natural breaks of the smoothed index. A series of the scenes of the last twelve months (the least cloudy of each month, with a scene accepted when at least 30\,\% of the field is clear) gives for each cell a stability, a change since the previous scene and a trend, and groups the cells into three to six monitoring zones.

Open data complete the site: the water features around the field (OpenStreetMap) and their distance, the elevation and slope (Copernicus DEM), and the soil texture (SoilGrids; \citealt{Poggio2021}). The suite suggests where to take the water from and which irrigation system suits the field, with the reasons. If a service cannot be reached, the program says what it could not reach and uses the simulated indices of the suite, which mix the reflectance of crop and soil by the crop cover of the daily model.

\subsection{Zone-by-zone irrigation, report and prescriptions}
For each management zone the FAO-56 root-zone balance \citep{Allen1998} is run with the zone's soil (SoilGrids at its centre, the nearest soil of the suite), its observed cover (the crop development of the daily model scaled by the zone's NDVI against the field's) and the weather in use, which gives its own dates and depths of irrigation and the volume compared with that of one depth for the whole field. The result goes into a field report (PDF) and into prescription files (GeoJSON and CSV with the zone, date, net and gross depth of each cell). Further tools of the same panel are described in the next subsection.

\subsection{Terrain, ephemeral gullies, soil loss and sensors}
The terrain of the field and its surroundings is read from open elevation tiles (Terrarium tiles of AWS Terrain Tiles, which come from SRTM, Copernicus and national lidar where available), smoothed, and its sinks are filled by priority flood; D8 flow directions and the upslope area $A$ give the channels, the watershed of any point and a hillshade, which the program draws as contours with flow arrows and as a three-dimensional surface. Ephemeral gullies are predicted where the local slope $S$ and the contributing area pass the topographic threshold of cultivated fields, $S=0.025\,A^{-0.4}$ \citep{Vandaele1996}, and run down the flow lines; the satellite photograph is then searched along the predicted lines for linear features with a Hessian ridge and valley filter \citep{Frangi1998}, and a line that has such a feature is marked as seen in the photograph. This is classical image analysis and not a trained network. Soil loss is estimated with the revised universal soil loss equation, $A_{\mathrm{loss}}=R\,K\,LS\,C\,P$, in which the rainfall erosivity $R$ comes from the annual rain \citep{Renard1994}, the erodibility $K$ from the soil texture and organic carbon \citep{Sharpley1990}, the slope-length factor $LS$ from the upslope area and the slope \citep{Moore1992}, the cover factor $C$ from the NDVI of the field \citep{vanderKnijff1999}, and $P=1$ unless a conservation practice is chosen. These tools were run on the test image and the test answers of the data services only; they were not compared with a mapped gully or a measured loss.

\section{Crops}
The library has 48 crops, 19 field crops (cereals, roots and tubers, legumes, oil crops, cotton) with a crop coefficient for the initial, mid-season and late-season stages, four stage lengths, height, rooting depth, the depletion fraction $p$ and, where tabulated, the yield response factor $K_y$ \citep{Doorenbos1979,Allen1998}, and 29 perennial crops (tree, vine and berry crops) with several planting densities or training systems, each with its ground cover, height, mid-season and end-season coefficient. The daily crop coefficient follows the piecewise curve of FAO-56,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kc}
K_c(t)=\begin{cases}K_{ini}, & t<L_1\\ K_{ini}+(K_{mid}-K_{ini})\dfrac{t-L_1}{L_2}, & L_1\le t<L_1+L_2\\ K_{mid}, & L_1+L_2\le t<L_1+L_2+L_3\\ K_{mid}+(K_{end}-K_{mid})\min\!\left(1,\dfrac{t-L_1-L_2-L_3}{L_4}\right), & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
which for evergreen crops is repeated each year. Every coefficient has a source field; where the root depth or the stages are not tabulated for a crop the group default is used and marked (11 crops); the yield response factor is tabulated for 11 crops and not tabulated for 37.

\section{Deterministic phenotyping}
The phenotyping layer is a virtual experiment. One plant of a chosen crop (modelled as maize, sorghum, wheat or common bean) and genotype grows for 140 days after emergence in the soil, tillage, weather and management of the project, twice: well-watered and under a drought treatment (a pulse, a terminal, an intermittent or no drought). The model has no random numbers, so the same controls always give the same plant. Light capture and biomass follow \citet{Monteith1977}, limited by transpiration as in \citet{Tanner1983}; reference evapotranspiration is FAO-56 Penman--Monteith \citep{Monteith1965,Allen1998}; soil water uses the van Genuchten curve \citep{vanGenuchten1980} and root uptake the stress function of \citet{Feddes1978}; leaf water potential, turgor and expansion follow a pressure--volume curve and \citet{Lockhart1965}, stomata \citet{Jarvis1976}, canopy temperature and the crop water stress index \citet{Jackson1981}, and the root system grows by rules after \citet{Pages2014}, with carbon allocated by functional equilibrium. The traits that are read out (projected shoot area, convex hull, solidity, senescence, canopy temperature depression, relative water content, root angle, depth and length) are those defined in the two books on phenotyping that the layer starts from \citep{Lorence2022,Panguluri2013}, which define the traits and not the equations. The constants of four species and seven genotypes are typical and editable values of the layer, not data of an experiment. The layer reaches the project: the crop and its library values, the soil, the weather and the toolbar date (which sets the season and a drought outlook for the next days), the mulch, the irrigation and the nitrogen rate. The user can load measured traits, compare, calibrate and export them.

\section{What was tested}
\subsection{Methods}
\paragraph{All layers.} The application was opened in Chromium (Playwright) and each of the 22 layers was opened, run through the Run button (or the Run simulation button of the design layers) and read: errors of the page script were recorded, the text of the results tab and the number of charts in the results and the charts tabs were counted. The layers with Daisy output were opened in a fresh application to see their empty state.

\paragraph{Crop library.} For each of the 110 combinations of crop and density the library gives parameters; the constraints tested were: $K_{mid}\ge K_{ini}$, $0.3<K_{mid}<1.4$, positive stages, a season length of 60 to 365 days, $0.1<p<0.8$, $0.2<K_y<1.6$ where tabulated, a root depth of 20 to 400\,cm, a daily $K_c$ curve whose maximum equals the largest of the three coefficients, and no daily jump of more than 0.05.

\paragraph{Phenotyping model.} The model was run in the application with a patched entry point that records every run the layer makes (56 runs when the layer opens), and directly for maize in a warm and in a hot, dry season with the seven genotypes, well-watered and under a drought pulse (onset day 40, 28 days at 30\,\% of the plant-available water, rain-out shelter): 28 runs, each run twice. Determinism is the maximum absolute difference between all stored daily series of the two runs. The soil water balance is $S_{end}-(S_0+P+I-T-E_s-D)$ with the totals of the run.

\subsection{Results}
\paragraph{Layers.} All 22 layers opened and ran with no error of the page script. The results tab of each layer had a text of 1\,346 to 3\,419 characters (median 1\,670; the phenotyping layer shows its results on the picture and in its own sections) and four to 16 charts; the three design layers have eight in the charts tab, the seasonal layers 16, 10 and 6 (yield reliability, full water balance, nitrogen budget). The Daisy layers showed no figure before a Daisy run, as designed (Paper~3).

\paragraph{Crop library.} Of the 110 combinations, 97 satisfy all constraints. The 13 that do not are evergreen tree crops (citrus in six densities, olive in six, date palm in one young planting) for which the library gives a mid-season coefficient below the initial coefficient, which for evergreen crops is the end-of-season value; the youngest olive plantings also have a mid-season coefficient of 0.25 to 0.30, below the general range. These values follow the source (a low ground cover of young trees) and are not errors of the code, but the layer draws a curve that falls from the initial to the mid-season value, which is unusual and is stated here.

\paragraph{Phenotyping.} The 56 runs of the layer and the 28 runs of the experiment were identical when repeated (maximum difference 0 in every stored series), and the soil water balance closed with a largest residual of $4\times10^{-5}$\,mm. In the warm season the drought pulse reduced the grain mass by 27 to 42\,\% and in the hot, dry season by 14 to 42\,\% (\cref{tab:pheno}, \cref{fig:pheno}). The genotype with the largest loss in both seasons is the shallow, dense one (42\,\%), whose root system reaches 109 to 84\,cm instead of 120\,cm; the water saver, which closes its stomata early, has the smallest absolute yield but one of the smallest losses (27 and 14\,\%), and the deep-rooting genotype loses the least in the hot season among those with a full yield (15\,\%). The osmotic adjuster gives the same grain mass as the reference in this setting: its adjustment changes the water potential and not the dry matter of the pulse. These are the contrasts that the model was built to show, and they follow from its equations; they are not predictions of real genotypes, whose constants are those of the layer.

\paragraph{Daisy.} The tests of the Daisy parts are in Paper~3: the compact designs of the experiments, the soil-column solver, the parsers of the logs, the chemical budget, and the denitrification function, which reproduces 792 stored Daisy outputs after the correction of one defect found by the test.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What the suite offers.} A person can go from a point on the map to a drawn field, to satellite indices and zones, to a design, a schedule, a simulation of the options and a report, in one window and with the same soil, crop and weather throughout, and can follow every number to an equation and its origin.

\paragraph{What the tests do and do not show.} They show that the suite starts, runs, is internally consistent in the places tested, and that the parts that were compared with outside references agree. They do not show that the suite is right for a field. In particular: (i) \emph{no field measurement} is used anywhere; the verification is against worked examples, held-out simulations, independent solvers and stored model output (Papers~2 and~3). (ii) \emph{The data services} (Sentinel-2, OpenStreetMap, elevation, SoilGrids, online weather) could not be reached in the environment of the tests; the pictures of the manual and of \cref{fig:field} were made on generated test images and test answers, so the reading of real scenes and the handling of cloud were not tested here. (iii) The layer sweep checks that each layer runs and produces output, not that every number is right. (iv) The phenotyping model is a model of the forms of the literature with typical constants and has not been fitted or tested against phenotyping data. (v) The crop coefficients are those of FAO-56 and of a review; a local calibration is the user's task. (vi) The test of the interface was made in one browser engine; the Windows application and its installer were not tested here. (vii) The largest class of equations (287 of the labelled) is engineered and has only the tests of this and the earlier papers.

\paragraph{Why traceability.} The cost of the rule is the work of writing it down; the benefit is that a disagreement with the suite can be located, and that a reader who disagrees with a choice can change it. Release 1.1.0 found three defects of the first release and one defect of the denitrification function by testing; none of them was visible on the screen.

\section{Conclusions}
SWAFL Desktop 1.1.0 is a single offline application that joins the field, the satellite, the design, the schedule, the process model and the plant, with traceability of its equations. The release was tested layer by layer, and the tests that could be made (all layers run, crop library, deterministic phenotyping, the Daisy parts) pass or show a stated limit. What remains is the comparison with measurements on real fields, the test of the data services on a connected machine, and the test of the installer on Windows; each is a defined piece of work and none is claimed.
